Working papers Publications

Working papers

  1. Audit Regulation for Financially Constrained Firms

    with Negin Attar-Niggemann and Felix Niggemann · 2026

    Stricter audit standards can increase financially constrained firms’ investment capacity despite raising audit fees.

  2. Effects of Revealing Client Names in PCAOB Inspection Reports on Private Litigation, Audit Effort, and Auditor Conservatism

    with Negin Attar-Niggemann and Felix Niggemann · 2026

    Naming clients in PCAOB inspection reports can improve audit compliance yet reduce welfare by encouraging conservatism that blocks profitable investment.

  3. Effects of Information Acquisition Costs on Corporate Investment and Surplus

    with Hyun Hwang and Eunhee Kim · 2026

    Lower costs for traders to acquire information about firms can reduce corporate investment and total surplus by increasing the trading losses borne by investors who provide firms’ capital.

  4. Financing Constraints, Performance Manipulation, and Founder Replacement

    2026

    Financing constraints make generous founder exit packages unaffordable, encouraging performance manipulation and leading to too little replacement by professional managers.

Publications

  1. Macroeconomic Implications of Accounting Standards

    with Hyun Hwang and Eunhee Kim

    Journal of Accounting and Economics · Forthcoming

    Conservative accounting standards can lower financing costs by reducing the number of firms seeking capital, while liberal standards can raise investors’ returns by expanding demand for funding.

  2. Financial Climate-Risk Measurement, Impact Funds, and Green Transitions

    with Lucas Mahieux

    Journal of Accounting Research · 64 (2026), 1867-1903

    Requiring precise climate-risk measurement can reduce green transitions by limiting the subsidies firms can obtain from investors seeking environmental impact.

  3. What Role do Boards Play in Companies with Visionary CEOs?

    with Xu Jiang

    Journal of Accounting Research · 62 (2024), 981-1005

    As a CEO’s confidence in their vision grows, the board shifts from advising to monitoring to rubberstamping, because imposing an alternative strategy can undermine the CEO’s motivation to implement it.

  4. Accounting Conservatism and Managerial Information Acquisition

    with Christian Laux

    Journal of Accounting and Economics · 77 (2024), 101630

    Conservative accounting can improve debt contracting because managers seeking to keep projects alive have stronger incentives to uncover evidence that overturns false alarms than to uncover problems hidden by optimistic reports.

  5. Early-Warning Signals and Risk-Shifting Incentives

    with Ronghuo Zheng

    The Accounting Review · 98 (2023), 273-288

    Conservative reporting can curb excessive risk-taking by threatening empire builders with project liquidation, while liberal reporting can do so by leaving managers who prefer a quiet life responsible for running failing projects.

  6. Signaling Private Information via Accounting System Design

    with Aysa Dordzhieva and Ronghuo Zheng

    Journal of Accounting and Economics · 74 (2022), 101494

    Adopting a liberal accounting system can signal favorable long-term prospects and thereby lower financing costs, even though overstated reports can lead investors to fund expansions whose expected benefits fall short of their costs.

  7. Managerial Optimism and Debt Covenants

    with Jakob Infuehr

    Journal of Accounting Research · 60 (2022), 353-371

    Optimistic managers accept tighter debt covenants because giving lenders more opportunities to recover funds through liquidation lowers the repayment required when projects succeed, the outcome these managers consider more likely.

  8. Debt Covenants and Accounting Manipulation

    The Accounting Review · 97 (2022), 293–314

    Debt covenants can keep poor projects alive when managers hide bad news to retain control and then resist renegotiation because shutting down the project would expose their earlier misreporting.

  9. Conservative Accounting, Audit Quality, and Litigation

    with Sebastian Kronenberger

    Management Science · 68 (2022), 2349-2362

    Greater litigation risk can lead to less conservative reporting when auditing is inexpensive, because it also strengthens auditors’ incentives to verify financial reports.

  10. Effects of Accounting Conservatism on Investment Efficiency and Innovation

    with Korok Ray

    Journal of Accounting and Economics · 70 (2020), 101319

    Conservative accounting can encourage innovation by making favorable earnings reports more reliable, allowing boards to reward managers more strongly for developing new ideas while discouraging them from investing in projects with poor prospects.

  11. Accounting Standards, Regulatory Enforcement, and Innovation

    with Phillip Stocken

    Journal of Accounting and Economics · 65 (2018), 221-236

    Stronger enforcement calls for stricter accounting standards when penalties vary little with violation size, but for looser standards when penalties are sensitive to it.

  12. Supporting and Assessing Agents

    Journal of Accounting Research · 55 (2017), 995-1016

    Providing employees with extra resources and freedom can reveal their abilities more clearly, strengthen career incentives, and reduce the need for monitoring and bonuses.

  13. Insider versus Outsider CEOs, Executive Compensation, and Accounting Manipulation

    with Prasart Jongjaroenkamol

    Journal of Accounting and Economics · 63 (2017), 253–261

    Boards are more likely to recruit outside CEOs when reported performance is harder to manipulate, because reliable reports help them identify and replace unsuccessful hires, limiting the greater downside risk of appointing an outsider.

  14. Corporate Governance, Accounting Conservatism, and Manipulation

    with Judson Caskey

    Management Science · 63 (2017), 424-437

    Stronger reporting oversight can improve investment efficiency while increasing accounting manipulation, because firms respond by adopting more conservative reporting systems.

  15. Executive Pay, Innovation, and Risk-Taking

    Journal of Economics & Management Strategy · 24 (2015), 272–301

    Stock options encourage CEOs to search for innovative ideas, while restricted stock curbs excessive risk-taking and severance pay reduces the fear of dismissal that can deter worthwhile risky investments.

  16. Pay Convexity, Earnings Manipulation, and Project Continuation

    The Accounting Review · 89 (2014), 2233-2259

    Stock options can encourage CEOs to manipulate interim reports to keep risky projects alive, even when their compensation depends entirely on long-term firm performance.

  17. Corporate Governance, Board Oversight, and CEO Turnover

    Foundations and Trends in Accounting · 8 (2014), 1-73

    This review connects CEO assessment and replacement to executive pay, board monitoring, investment decisions, financial reporting, and the selection of new leaders.

  18. Managerial Reporting, Overoptimism, and Litigation Risk

    with Phillip Stocken

    Journal of Accounting and Economics · 53 (2012), 577-591

    Higher damages for misreporting can lower financing costs by increasing investors’ expected compensation if the firm fails, making misreporting to obtain funding more attractive to optimistic entrepreneurs who consider failure unlikely.

  19. Stock Option Vesting Conditions, CEO Turnover, and Myopic Investment

    Journal of Financial Economics · 106 (2012), 513-526

    Longer option vesting periods can encourage short-term investment because CEOs seek early successes to avoid dismissal and the loss of their unvested options.

  20. Board Independence, Executive Pay, and the Adoption of Pet Projects

    with Brian Mittendorf

    Contemporary Accounting Research · 28 (2011), 1467-1483

    Boards aligned with the CEO can discourage pet projects by offering stronger performance incentives, although doing so may increase the CEO’s compensation.

  21. Effects of Litigation Risk on Board Oversight and CEO Incentive Pay

    Management Science · 56 (2010), 938-948

    When monitoring is costly, greater director liability can lead boards to reduce performance-based CEO pay, weakening the CEO’s incentive to manipulate earnings and thereby leading boards to monitor less.

  22. On the Benefits of Allowing CEOs to Time Their Stock Option Exercises

    RAND Journal of Economics · 41 (2010), 118-138

    Allowing CEOs to choose when to exercise stock options can improve decisions to abandon weak projects by reducing options’ bias toward continued investment.

  23. Auditor Liability and Client Acceptance Decisions

    with Paul Newman

    The Accounting Review · 85 (2010), 261-285

    Stronger auditor liability can reduce client rejections because better audits make clients willing to pay higher fees that encourage careful evaluation, but very strict liability can make litigation expenses outweigh these benefits.

  24. Board Committees, CEO Compensation, and Earnings Management

    with Christian Laux

    The Accounting Review · 84 (2009), 869-891

    Assigning compensation and audit responsibilities to separate board committees can increase stock-based CEO pay because the compensation committee does not bear the additional oversight costs that such pay imposes on the audit committee.

  25. Board Independence and CEO Turnover

    Journal of Accounting Research · 46 (2008), 137-171

    More independent boards are more willing to dismiss CEOs, but must offer larger severance payments to elicit honest information about their suitability for the job and larger stock option grants to offset the resulting weaker effort incentives.

  26. On the Value of Influence Activities for Capital Budgeting

    Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization · 65 (2008), 625-635

    Requiring managers to spend effort advocating for projects can improve capital allocation by encouraging them to gather and reveal information about project prospects.

  27. The Ignored Performance Measure

    Journal of Economics & Management Strategy · 15 (2006), 707-733

    An informative performance measure can be worthless for incentive contracting when a manager must both evaluate a project and implement it.

  28. Performance Measurement and Information Production

    with Christian Laux

    Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics JITE · 162 (2006), 412-423

    Imperfect performance measures can make weaker performance-based pay desirable because strong incentives encourage employees to spend effort learning how to game the measures.
