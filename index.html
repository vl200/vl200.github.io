Working papers Publications

Working papers

  1. Audit Regulation for Financially Constrained Firms

    with Negin Attar-Niggemann and Felix Niggemann · 2026

    Optimal audit standards become stricter as financing constraints tighten, because stricter audits raise the income firms can credibly promise investors, and the added financing is worth most where underinvestment is most severe.

  2. Effects of Revealing Client Names in PCAOB Inspection Reports on Private Litigation, Audit Effort, and Auditor Conservatism

    with Negin Attar-Niggemann and Felix Niggemann · 2026

    Naming clients in PCAOB inspection reports can improve audit compliance but may reduce welfare by encouraging conservatism that blocks profitable investment.

  3. Effects of Information Acquisition Costs on Corporate Investment and Surplus

    with Hyun Hwang and Eunhee Kim · 2026

    Lower costs for traders to acquire information about firms can reduce corporate investment and total surplus by increasing the trading losses borne by investors who provide firms’ capital.

  4. Financing Constraints, Performance Manipulation, and Founder Replacement

    2026

    Financing constraints can lead to too little, not too much, founder replacement, because founders who must accept smaller exit packages to raise capital manipulate the performance metrics investors use to decide whether to replace them.

Publications

  1. Macroeconomic Implications of Accounting Standards

    with Hyun Hwang and Eunhee Kim

    Journal of Accounting and Economics · Forthcoming

    Conservative accounting standards can lower financing costs by reducing the number of firms seeking capital, while liberal standards can raise investors’ returns by expanding demand for funding.

  2. Financial Climate-Risk Measurement, Impact Funds, and Green Transitions

    with Lucas Mahieux

    Journal of Accounting Research · 64 (2026), 1867–1903

    Requiring precise climate-risk measurement can reduce green transitions, because when measurement is vague, impact investors cannot tell which firms would go green anyway, so they subsidize more broadly and more firms make the transition.

  3. What Role Do Boards Play in Companies with Visionary CEOs?

    with Xu Jiang

    Journal of Accounting Research · 62 (2024), 981–1005

    As a CEO’s confidence in their vision grows, the board shifts from advising to monitoring to rubberstamping, because imposing an alternative strategy can undermine the CEO’s motivation to implement it.

  4. Accounting Conservatism and Managerial Information Acquisition

    with Christian Laux

    Journal of Accounting and Economics · 77 (2024), 101630

    Conservative accounting can be optimal for borrowing firms, because managers who want to keep their projects alive search harder for evidence that overturns bad reports than for evidence that overturns good ones.

  5. Early-Warning Signals and Risk-Shifting Incentives

    with Ronghuo Zheng

    The Accounting Review · 98 (2023), 273–288

    Managers who borrow can commit to prudent investment by adopting a reporting system that sends too many or too few early warnings, because the resulting mistaken project decisions hurt them most when they take excessive risks.

  6. Signaling Private Information via Accounting System Design

    with Aysa Dordzhieva and Ronghuo Zheng

    Journal of Accounting and Economics · 74 (2022), 101494

    Firms with strong long-term prospects can lower financing costs by adopting a reporting system that tends to overstate performance, a signal weaker firms will not copy because the inefficient expansions it triggers cost them more.

  7. Managerial Optimism and Debt Covenants

    with Jakob Infuehr

    Journal of Accounting Research · 60 (2022), 353–371

    Optimistic managers may choose tighter debt covenants, even though lenders will then liquidate more often, because letting lenders recover more through liquidation lowers the repayment owed if the project succeeds, an outcome optimists overrate.

  8. Debt Covenants and Accounting Manipulation

    The Accounting Review · 97 (2022), 293–314

    Debt covenants can lead to fewer, not more, liquidations of poor projects, because managers hide bad news to retain control and then resist renegotiation, since shutting down the project would expose their earlier misreporting.

  9. Conservative Accounting, Audit Quality, and Litigation

    with Sebastian Kronenberger

    Management Science · 68 (2022), 2349–2362

    Greater litigation risk can lead firms to choose less conservative reporting systems when auditing is inexpensive, because auditors facing more liability check reports more carefully, reducing the need for conservatism as a safeguard against overstatement.

  10. Effects of Accounting Conservatism on Investment Efficiency and Innovation

    with Korok Ray

    Journal of Accounting and Economics · 70 (2020), 101319

    Conservative accounting can encourage innovation by making favorable earnings reports more reliable, allowing boards to reward managers more strongly for developing new ideas while discouraging them from investing in projects with poor prospects.

  11. Accounting Standards, Regulatory Enforcement, and Innovation

    with Phillip Stocken

    Journal of Accounting and Economics · 65 (2018), 221–236

    Stronger enforcement can call for stricter accounting standards when penalties vary little with violation size, but for looser standards when penalties are sensitive to it.

  12. Supporting and Assessing Agents

    Journal of Accounting Research · 55 (2017), 995–1016

    Firms may give employees more resources and freedom than the direct returns justify, because support lets talented employees stand out, so promotions can rely on performance rather than costly monitoring, strengthening motivation and lowering incentive pay.

  13. Insider versus Outsider CEOs, Executive Compensation, and Accounting Manipulation

    with Prasart Jongjaroenkamol

    Journal of Accounting and Economics · 63 (2017), 253–261

    Boards may be more willing to hire outside CEOs when reported performance is harder to manipulate, because reliable reports help them identify and replace unsuccessful hires, limiting the greater downside risk of appointing an outsider.

  14. Corporate Governance, Accounting Conservatism, and Manipulation

    with Judson Caskey

    Management Science · 63 (2017), 424–437

    Stronger reporting oversight can improve investment decisions yet increase manipulation: boards with better oversight adopt more conservative accounting, which gives managers seeking approval for expansions a stronger reason to inflate reports.

  15. Executive Pay, Innovation, and Risk-Taking

    Journal of Economics & Management Strategy · 24 (2015), 275–305

    Stock options can encourage CEOs to search for innovative ideas, and the optimal pay package adds restricted stock when CEOs are prone to excessive risk-taking or severance pay when fear of dismissal makes them too cautious.

  16. Pay Convexity, Earnings Manipulation, and Project Continuation

    The Accounting Review · 89 (2014), 2233–2259

    Stock options can encourage CEOs to manipulate interim reports to keep risky projects alive, even when their compensation depends entirely on long-term firm performance.

  17. Corporate Governance, Board Oversight, and CEO Turnover

    Foundations and Trends in Accounting · 8 (2014), 1–73

    This review synthesizes research showing that, because boards must evaluate and possibly replace CEOs, practices that look like governance failures, such as limited monitoring, severance pay, or short-term investment, can be optimal.

  18. Managerial Reporting, Overoptimism, and Litigation Risk

    with Phillip Stocken

    Journal of Accounting and Economics · 53 (2012), 577–591

    Higher damages for misreporting can increase misreporting, because investors who expect compensation if the firm fails demand a smaller stake, so optimistic entrepreneurs, who consider failure unlikely, gain more from hiding bad news to secure funding.

  19. Stock Option Vesting Conditions, CEO Turnover, and Myopic Investment

    Journal of Financial Economics · 106 (2012), 513–526

    Longer option vesting periods, often proposed as a cure for short-termism, can instead encourage excessive short-term investment, because CEOs chase early successes to avoid being fired and forfeiting their unvested options.

  20. Board Independence, Executive Pay, and the Adoption of Pet Projects

    with Brian Mittendorf

    Contemporary Accounting Research · 28 (2011), 1467–1483

    A board that is not fully independent of the CEO can benefit shareholders, because it offers larger performance bonuses, which steer the CEO away from pet projects and motivate a search for better ones.

  21. Effects of Litigation Risk on Board Oversight and CEO Incentive Pay

    Management Science · 56 (2010), 938–948

    Greater director liability can lead boards to monitor less, not more, because when monitoring is costly, directors limit their exposure mainly by cutting performance-based CEO pay, which weakens the CEO’s incentive to manipulate earnings.

  22. On the Benefits of Allowing CEOs to Time Their Stock Option Exercises

    RAND Journal of Economics · 41 (2010), 118–138

    Allowing CEOs to time option exercises based on inside information can improve decisions to abandon weak projects, because a CEO who has cashed out after bad news no longer loses option value when the project is dropped.

  23. Auditor Liability and Client Acceptance Decisions

    with Paul Newman

    The Accounting Review · 85 (2010), 261–285

    Stronger auditor liability can reduce client rejections because better audits make clients willing to pay higher fees that encourage careful evaluation, but very strict liability can make litigation expenses outweigh these benefits.

  24. Board Committees, CEO Compensation, and Earnings Management

    with Christian Laux

    The Accounting Review · 84 (2009), 869–891

    Assigning compensation and audit responsibilities to separate board committees can raise both stock-based CEO pay and board oversight, because a compensation committee that does not bear monitoring costs grants more stock-based pay, which prompts the audit committee to monitor more.

  25. Board Independence and CEO Turnover

    Journal of Accounting Research · 46 (2008), 137–171

    Full board independence may not be in shareholders’ interest, because once the CEO has invested in firm-specific skills, an independent board offers larger severance pay so the CEO steps down more often, and CEOs who anticipate this invest less.

  26. On the Value of Influence Activities for Capital Budgeting

    Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization · 65 (2008), 625–635

    Requiring managers to spend costly, even unproductive, effort lobbying for projects can improve capital allocation, because managers will bear that cost only for projects they expect to succeed and will investigate prospects first.

  27. The Ignored Performance Measure

    Journal of Economics & Management Strategy · 15 (2006), 707–733

    An informative measure of a manager’s implementation effort can be useless for incentive pay, because when the manager must also evaluate the project, the compensation risk that motivates careful evaluation may already ensure diligent implementation.

  28. Performance Measurement and Information Production

    with Christian Laux

    Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics · 162 (2006), 412–423

    When pay depends on an imperfect performance measure, employees can spend too much effort learning how to game it, so firms may prefer weaker incentives that discourage that learning, not just the gaming itself.
